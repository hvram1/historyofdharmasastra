45. Pulastya

Volume I: Part I pp. 516–517

Pulastya is established as an important expounder of dharma, a figure whose name is notably mentioned in a verse from Vṛddha Yājñavalkya. His influence is evident through the widespread citations found in the works of later, authoritative legal commentators. For instance, Viśvarūpa, as well as the renowned authors of the Mitākṣarā and Aparārka, extensively quote numerous verses attributed to Pulastya. These particular citations delve into intricate details concerning daily observances, or *āhnika*, and the crucial ancestral rites known as *śrāddha*, underscoring his contribution to practical ritual law. Furthermore, the *Dānanīratnākara* cites a distinct prose passage identified as Pulastya's, suggesting a diverse textual legacy beyond mere versification. Consequently, scholarly analysis places the period of composition for the works ascribed to Pulastya between the fourth and seventh centuries A.D., situating his contributions within a formative era of Dharmaśāstra literature.

— Synopsis by P.V. Kane, from History of Dharmaśāstra

45. Pulastya

45. Pulastya

Pulastya's name is among the ten primordial sages men- tioned in the Manusmṛti I. 34-35.

Pulastya is one of the expounders of dharma enumerated by Vṛddha-Yājñavalkya. Viśvarūpa quotes a verse from him on śarīraśauca.1 The Mit. (on Yāj. I. 261) cites a verse from Pulastya that a Brāhmaṇa should principally use ascetic's food (i. e. vegetable food) in śrāddha, that kṣatriyas and vaiśyas should use meat and śūdras madhu.2 The Mit. (Yāj. III. 253) quotes two verses of Pulastya who enumerates eleven intoxicating drinks together with surā as the twelfth.3 Aparārka quotes several verses from Pulastya on saṁdhyā, śrāddha, āśauca, duties of yatis, prāyaścitta. Aparārka quotes two verses from Pulastya propounding the view that a combination of jñāna and karma is the correct view.4 The first of these verses is ascribed by him to Yoga-Yājñavalkya elsewhere (note 336). The Smṛticandrikā quotes about forty verses from Pulastya on āhnika and śrāddha. In one place it quotes Pulastya on the efficacy of bathing on Sunday, Tuesday, and Saturday.5 In another place it refers to the japa of Rāma, Paraśurāma, Nṛsimha Trivikrama.

Aparārka (on p. 1136) quotes eight verses from Pulastya that provide various religious rites or actions for dog-bite in the case of brāhmaṇa men and womeṇ

The Dānarātnākara of Cāṇḍeśvara cites a prose text from Pulastya on the gift of deer-skiṇ6

The Pulastya-smṛti must have been composed between 4th and 7th century A. D.
