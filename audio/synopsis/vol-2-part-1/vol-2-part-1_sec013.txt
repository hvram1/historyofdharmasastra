Chapter Thirteen delves into the complex practice of Niyoga. There exists a great divergence of views concerning its origin and fundamental purpose within the dharmaśāstra tradition. Smṛtikāras, recognizing its sensitive nature, laid down extremely stringent conditions for niyoga to be resorted to, with any breach of these conditions being severely condemned and made punishable. Interestingly, some very ancient writers on dharma did not allow this practice at all, indicating early dissent.

Despite these reservations, the Mahābhārata offers numerous examples of niyoga in practice. Later interpretations further refined its scope; some writers posited that texts permitting niyoga applied primarily to śūdras, or specifically to girls who were only promised in marriage to a person but not actually married to him, particularly if the prospective husband had died prematurely. A significant legal and social question that arose was 'to whom the child born of niyoga belonged,' a matter on which three distinct views emerged. Ultimately, Niyoga was expressly forbidden in the Kali age by Bṛhaspati and several other smṛti writers, marking its eventual cessation.
