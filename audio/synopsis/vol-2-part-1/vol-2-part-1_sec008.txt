The chapter delves into the origin and development of the profound concept of āśramas, the prescribed stages of life. It establishes that the number of āśramas has been recognised as four since the times of the most ancient dharmasūtras, a framework prominently elaborated in Manu's theory. Tracing their historical roots, it highlights that the brahmacarya and householder's stage were already well-known even to the Ṛgveda. Furthermore, Vedic Literature contains references to figures like the Vaikhānasa and yati, foreshadowing later stages of renunciation. Interestingly, the Chāndogya Upaniṣad expressly mentions three āśramas, suggesting an evolving understanding of these life-stages.

The overarching philosophical purpose of the āśrama system is inextricably linked to the idea of mokṣa, signifying release from saṁsāra. This structural framework operates in a complementary relationship with the varṇa system, together forming the bedrock of social and spiritual order. The text further explores the nuances within this system, noting that despite the general consensus on four āśramas, there exist three different points of view regarding their interpretation and application.
