The study of marriage begins by noting the distinct absence of polyandry in Sanskrit Literature, with the singular exception of the narrative involving Draupadi. A wife's primary dharma involved active cooperation with her husband in all religious matters, for she was not authorised to perform such rites independently or without his express consent. In polygamous households, a specific precedence among co-wives governed these religious observances. This role was intimately linked to the theory of inherent debts every man was born with, notably the debt to his ancestors, discharged primarily through the procreation of sons. The duties of a wife are extensively elaborated across various smṛtis and digests, consistently highlighting obedience and reverence for the husband as a god as her foremost obligations. This rigorous adherence defined the ideal of a pativratā, whose conduct was meticulously prescribed even when her husband was away on a journey, with supernatural powers often ascribed to such exemplary devotion.

Despite these stringent duties, a wife was entitled to rights such as residence and maintenance. The husband, in turn, possessed a power of correction, though even in cases of severe transgression like adultery, humane treatment was mandated. Significantly, for secular or legal purposes, the husband and wife did not possess identical identities. Reflecting on the position of women in ancient Indiā, Sanskrit works present a complex estimate of female character. While certain passages condemn women's character, this is balanced by a high eulogy of and profound reverence for the mother, indicating a nuanced societal perspective.
