The chapter on Upanayana explores its meaning, origin, and development as a pivotal saṁskāra, inherently implying gāyatryupadeśa. It details the ancient features of this originally simple ceremony, specifying the proper age for the three varṇas and the auspicious time. Rules for the brahmacārin’s skin, garments, girdle, and staff are outlined, alongside preliminary rites like homa and the principal observances. The history of yajñopavīta, including its manufacture, wearing, questions of women's upanayana, and its abandonment by kṣatriyas post-Christ, are discussed. The text also covers upanayana for the blind, deaf, dumb, idiots, mixed castes, and the aśvattha tree, culminating in the imparting of sacred Gāyatrī, Vyāhṛtis, 'om', and Gāyatrī's eulogy.

Dharmas of brahmacārins, including bhikṣā for food and the twice-daily performance of Saṁdhyā, are then presented, detailing elements like ācamana, prāṇāyāma, mārjana, aghamarṣaṇa, arghya to the sun, japa of Gāyatrī, upasthāna, Nyāsas, and Mudrās. Veda study forms the primary duty within the ancient educational system, characterized by oral instruction, teaching without stipulated fees, and student residence with the teacher. Qualifications for teachers and pupils, student work for the teacher, and rules for honouring teachers, elders, and women relatives are outlined, alongside courtesies and precedence on public roads. Duration of student-hood, subjects, corporal punishment, and education for kṣatriyas, vaiśyas, śūdras, and women, including merits and defects, are also examined.

Concluding sections cover the Veda-vratas, naiṣṭhika students, and patitasāvitrīka status. Debates on the existence of kṣatriyas and vaiśyas in the Kali age, the Vrātyastoma for those whose upanayana was not performed, absorption of foreign elements, tolerance in ancient India, and Punarupanayana are explored. Anadhyāya, Keśānta or Godāna, and Snāna or Samāvartana—the student's return from the teacher after Vedic study—are detailed, followed by the prescribed rules of conduct for snātakas.
