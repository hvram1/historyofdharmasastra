The duties prescribed for widows commenced with specific rules of conduct for one year following the husband's demise. During widowhood, a woman was enjoined to lead an ascetic life, abstaining from luxuries such as perfumes, flowers, and chewing betelnut. Societally, a widow—with the notable exception of one's own mother—was often declared to be most inauspicious. However, her rights in a joint family and as an heir to her husband's separate property were acknowledged, and it is observed that the widow's position has significantly improved through recent legislation.

Regarding the practice of tonsure for *brāhmaṇa* widows, a detailed examination reveals it has no sanction in the *vedas* and *smṛtis*, save for one or two isolated texts. Instead, this practice is largely insisted upon by the *Skandapurāṇa* and certain medieval digests, and it appears to have gradually evolved from approximately the 10th or 11th century. There was also a prevalent sentiment that a woman should not be killed on any account. More broadly, the position of women became assimilated to that of *śūdras* in religious matters, though certain advantages were, nevertheless, conceded to them. Furthermore, the practice of *purda* was not widespread among women generally; it was primarily limited to queens and ladies of high or noble rank.
