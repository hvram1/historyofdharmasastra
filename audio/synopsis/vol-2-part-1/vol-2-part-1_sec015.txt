The practice of Satī, eventually forbidden in India from 1829, was a form of widow burning observed not only in India but in many countries. Historically, its practice was quite limited in ancient times. The rite distinguished between *sahagamana* – accompanying the husband onto the pyre – and *anumarana*, where the widow followed later, with Brāhmaṇa widows specifically not allowed *anumarana*.

References to the practice are found in classical Sanskrit Literature and corroborated by epigraphic records, which often detail the spiritual rewards promised to a Satī. However, it is important to note that some commentators were vocally opposed to this practice, and the *smṛtis* themselves imposed specific restrictions against widow-burning. The chapter also elucidates the prescribed procedure of the rite of widow-burning.

Significantly, widow-burning was observed to be more prevalent in Bengal than elsewhere, a phenomenon largely attributed to the comparatively higher rights of succession granted to wives in that region.
