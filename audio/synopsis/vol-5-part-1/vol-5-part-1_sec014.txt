The conceptualization of Kāla, or time, in Indian thought is multifaceted, often posited as an independent tattva, or fundamental reality, or even as a manifestation of Brahman, an attribute of Isvara. Diverse philosophical schools offer distinct perspectives: the Nyaya-Vaisheshika systems articulate Kāla as an eternal, all-pervading substance, while Mimamsa primarily concerns itself with the practical application of time in ritual performance. Vedanta, conversely, often views Kāla as an aspect of Maya, illusory yet real within the phenomenal world.

In the realm of Dharmaśāstra, the understanding of Kāla holds profound practical significance, dictating auspicious times for various saṁskāra ceremonies and the precise timings for the execution of āśrama duties. Its importance is paramount in Vedic rituals and yajñas, where the efficacy of an act is intricately linked to its performance at a prescribed moment. The texts meticulously define units of Kāla, ranging from the infinitesimal nimeṣa to muhūrta, divasa, māsa, varṣa, and the vast cosmic scales of yuga and kalpa.

Furthermore, the discipline of jyotiṣa, or astrology, is deeply interwoven with the conception of Kāla, particularly in electional astrology for determining propitious muhūrta for undertakings. Kāla also factors critically into prāyaścitta, influencing the duration and effectiveness of penance. Key Smṛti texts like Manusmṛti and Yājñavalkya frequently reference Kāla, acknowledging its role not only in the variability of dharma across different yuga periods but also as a fundamental determinant in the structure and function of the cosmic order.
