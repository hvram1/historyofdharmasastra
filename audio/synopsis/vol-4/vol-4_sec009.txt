The chapter on Shrāddha commences with an exploration of its derivation and definition, linking the practice fundamentally to the doctrines of karma and punar-janma, and its profound significance for the pitṛs. The panegyrics surrounding śrāddha underscore its revered position within Hindu tradition. Historically, ancient times recognised primarily three rites for the departed: the piṇḍapitṛ-yajña, the mahāpitṛyajña, and the aṣṭakās. The text then elucidates the adhikārin, the person entitled to perform śrāddha, alongside a detailed examination of the meaning of the word ‘putra’ in this ritualistic context.

Further, the chapter meticulously specifies the proper times and places for śrāddha, presenting a comprehensive classification of the various types of śrāddhas. Crucial attention is dedicated to outlining the qualifications of brāhmaṇas suitable for invitation, contrasting these with persons deemed unfit to be invited. The discussion extends to the dietary regulations, detailing the food recommended and condemned in śrāddha, thus highlighting the intricate rules governing the ritual offering.

The overview elaborates on the specific ritual components, including the piṇḍa-pitṛyajña as described in the Taittirīya Saṃhitā and Brāhmaṇa. It proceeds to detail the pārvaṇaśrāddha as presented in the Āśvalāyanagṛhya, Yājñavalkya, and the Padmapurāṇa, addressing the roles of Viśve-devas and agnau-karaṇa. Specific numbers of āhutis and the varying numbers of ancestors to whom piṇḍas are offered – three, six, nine, or twelve – are discussed. The chapter concludes with a description of the modern pārvaṇaśrāddha and clarifies the meanings of the terms sapiṇḍa and āmaśrāddha.
