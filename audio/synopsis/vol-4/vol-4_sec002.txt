Chapter Two of P.V. Kane's authoritative *History of Dharmaśāstra* undertakes a comprehensive examination of the diverse means prescribed for reducing the consequences of sins. Foremost among these are the internal acts of sincere confession and genuine repentance, both deemed fundamental for the purification of the individual's conscience and spirit. These crucial introspective steps are often augmented by a range of more external and disciplined practices.

The chapter meticulously details such rigorous methods as *prāṇāyāma*, the systematic regulation of breath, and various forms of *tapas*, or severe ascetic self-discipline. Furthermore, it outlines the importance of ritualistic performances like *homa*, involving sacred oblations offered into a consecrated fire, and the spiritual efficacy of dedicated *japa*, the repeated muttering of sacred texts or mantras. Significant emphasis is also placed on charitable acts, particularly the giving of gifts, as a potent means of atonement and expiation.

Concluding the array of remedies, Kane discusses the profound impact of fasting in its different forms, along with the spiritual merit accrued through undertaking pilgrimages to holy sites. Each of these practices, from introspection to outward observance, plays a vital role in the elaborate system devised for mitigating the repercussions of transgression within the Dharmaśāstric tradition.
