The Nāradasmṛti, primarily focusing on *vyavahāra*, exists in both smaller and larger versions. Dr. Jolly’s edition incorporates Asahāya’s commentary, revised by Kalyāṇabhaṭṭa. Nārada is not listed among dharma expounders by Yājñavalkya or Parāśara, though Manu (I. 35) identifies him as one of the ten Prajāpatis, and he is a prominent figure in the Mahābhārata. Structurally, the text typically begins with three introductory chapters on judicial procedure and *sabhā*, followed by eighteen *vyavahārapadas*, and an appendix on theft, from a Nepal manuscript. The printed Nārada contains 1028 verses, with some 700 quoted in digests; quotations by Viśvarūpa and Medhātithi align with the printed text. A significant relationship with the Manusmṛti is evident, with 50 identical verses and many more closely corresponding, indicating Nārada's reliance on an extant Manu, despite certain differences. Some verses also agree with the Mahābhārata and Kauṭilya.

Nārada distinguishes itself through numerous subdivisions and unique topics, such as fourteen kinds of impotent persons, three *punarbhūs*, and four *svairiņīs*. It also propounds several juristic and political principles, including fixing the period of minority at sixteen years. Chronologically, Nārada is placed somewhat later than Yājñavalkya and definitively earlier than the 8th century, a timeframe supported by Bāṇa's reference to Nāradīya. Its mention of 'dināra' places it between 100-300 A.D., challenging Dr. Jolly's later dating based on this. Verses on *ācāra*, *śrāddha*, and *prāyaścitta* quoted elsewhere likely stem from a different Nārada. Bhavasvāmin, a Brāhmaṇa *bhāsyakara* from Kerala, commented on it, though his precise date is uncertain. Its home remains unascertained, with Dr. Jolly’s Nepal theory deemed speculative. The Mahābhārata quotes Nārada's views on flesh-eating and *utpātas*, while other texts like Jyotir-Nārada also exist.
