P.V. Kane presents Yājñavalkya as an illustrious Vedic sage, known from the Śatapathabrāhmaṇa and Bṛhadāraṇyaka, whose Smṛti claims authorship of the Āraṇyaka and Yogāśāstra. Textual analysis reveals variations in verse counts and arrangement, particularly in the prāyaścittakāṇḍa, among commentators Viśvarūpa, Mitākṣarā, and Aparārka. The Agnipurāṇa and Garuḍapurāṇa serve as crucial textual checks, both presenting intermediate versions between Viśvarūpa and Mitākṣarā; the Agnipurāṇa reflecting a text around 900 A.D., and the Garuḍapurāṇa, though claiming Yājñavalkya's dharma, omits the rājadharma section. Some Agnipurāṇa verses on vyavahāra derive from Nārada, yet the Yājñavalkya text appears largely consistent since 700 A.D., with little indication of multiple strata.

The Yājñavalkyasmṛti shows close phraseological agreement with the Manusmṛti, often compressing its dicta, but adds Vināyakāśānti, Grahaśānti, and detailed ordeals, unlike Manu. It also reflects a more advanced thought, notably prohibiting brāhmaṇa marriage to śūdra girls, and not condemning niyoga or gambling, unlike Manu. Intimately connected to the White Yajurveda and Pāraśaragṛhya, Dr. Jolly's dharmasūtra origin theory for the Smṛti is unfounded. Dated between 100 B.C. and 300 A.D., centuries before Viśvarūpa, theories of Greek astrological influence are untenable; Yājñavalkya refers only to nine planets and nakṣatras, not zodiacal signs or weekdays. Related works include Vṛddha-Yājñavalkya and Bṛhad-Yājñavalkya, and Kane lists principal commentaries by Viśvarūpa, Vijñāneśvara, Aparārka, Śūlapāṇi, and Mitramiśra.
