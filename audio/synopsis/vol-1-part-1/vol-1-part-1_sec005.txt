Gautama's is distinguished as the oldest extant dharmasūtra, bearing a close connection to the Sāmaveda and specifically studied by its followers. He belonged to the Rānāyaṇīya school of Sāmaveda, being one of its nine subdivisions, and the text itself frequently alludes to the author's own previous dicta. Comprising twenty-eight chapters, the entire work is presented in prose. Notably, Gautama's language exhibits greater agreement with Pāṇini's grammatical rules than that of Āpastamba, a linguistic characteristic that Haradatta, among others, preferred in his readings of Gautama's text. Intriguingly, certain sūtras attributed to Gautama are quoted in significant later works like the Mitākṣarā, yet remain absent from the extant version of his text. While Gautama's own work reveals a knowledge of extensive literature, Manu is the sole author named within it; the term ‘ācāryāh’ is often employed to cite other scholarly views.

The Baudhāyana-dharmasūtra offers the earliest direct references to Gautama on dharma, with Baudhāyana (III.10) borrowing chapter 19 and many other sūtras showing close correspondence. Vasiṣṭha (4.35 and 37) likewise refers to Gautama's views, with his 22nd chapter paralleling Gautama's 19th and many sūtras being identical. Gautama's enduring authority is attested by later references from Manu (identifying him as son of Utathya), Yājñavalkya, Bhaviṣyapurāṇa, Kumārila, Śaṁkarācārya, and Medhātithi. His mention of Yavana places its probable age between 600–400 B.C. The work's significance is underscored by commentaries from Haradatta, Maskarin, and Asahāya. It also presents special matters, occasionally incorporating śloka, requiring a distinction between Gautama and Vṛddha-Gautama.
