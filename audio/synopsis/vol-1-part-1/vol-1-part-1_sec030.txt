The term smṛti is understood in two principal senses: it refers broadly to all orthodox ancient non-Vedic works, encompassing texts like Pāṇini's grammar, Śrauta sūtras, and the Mahābhārata. Within the Manusmṛti itself, Manu Prajāpati is frequently referenced, appearing as a primeval figure among ten Prajāpatis. While scholarly critiques, such as those by Hopkins, exist concerning Manu, the text meticulously details the world's creation by Paramātman. Internal evidence, including mentions of human authors like Atri and Bhṛgu, and to dasyus speaking mleccha and Ārya languages, suggests the extant Manusmṛti is not older than 200 B.C. This dating is corroborated by external evidence from early commentators like Medhātithi—whose is the first extant commentary—and citations by Viśvarūpa, Śaṅkara, Kumārila, Śabara, Bṛhaspati, Aśvaghoṣa's Vajrasūci, and the Rāmāyaṇa. Thus, the extant Manusmṛti is generally dated to between the 2nd century B.C. and the 2nd century A.D.

The Manusmṛti exhibits earlier and later strata, evident in contradictory statements regarding a Brāhmaṇa marrying a śūdra woman, appropriate forms of marriage, niyoga, and flesh-eating. Bühler's analysis identifies cosmological and philosophical portions in the 1st and 12th books, and rules concerning mixed castes in the 10th book, as later additions, all integrated before the 3rd century A.D., though the text did not suffer multiple recasts. Vṛddha Manu and Bṛhan-Manu are considered later compositions. The relationship between the Mahābhārata and Manu is intricate, with conflicting scholarly views. While Manu mentions names and stories traceable to Vedic antiquities, it never explicitly names the Mahābhārata, which, in turn, often refers to 'rājadharma' or 'śāstra of Manu'. The final conclusion posits that a dharmasastra attributed to Svāyaṁbhuva Manu and a rājadharma work attributed to Pracetasa Manu existed long before the 4th century B.C., likely as one work, which was subsequently recast into the extant Manusmṛti between 200 B.C. and 200 A.D., with the extant Mahābhārata being a later composition.

The influence of Manu spread significantly, extending to countries like Cambodia beyond India. This pivotal text has attracted numerous commentators, including Medhātithi, Govindarāja, Kullūka, Nārāyaṇa, Rāghavānanda, Nandana, and Rāmachandra, alongside others such as Asahāya, Udayākara, and Dharaṇīdhara, who enriched its exegesis. Works cited as Vṛddha Manu and Bṛhan-Manu, if distinct, are understood to have been composed before the 7th century A.D.
