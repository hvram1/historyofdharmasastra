Despite its hundred chapters, the Viṣṇudharmasūtra is not extensive; several contain only a single sūtra and one verse. While its first and final two chapters are entirely in verse, the rest mix prose and verse. Closely connected with the Kāthakaśākhā, Dr. Jolly notes correspondences with the Kāthaka gṛhya, though the Viṣṇu is not by its author. Peculiarly, it claims divine revelation by God Viṣṇu, in an easy, diffuse style, resembling the Vas. Dh. S. The work blends old and new material; hundreds of its sūtras are prose renderings of verses from Manusmṛti, indicating direct borrowing. Verses are also shared with the Bhagavadgītā and Yajñavalkya smṛti, though Dr. Jolly's theory of Yajñavalkya borrowing anatomical sections from Viṣṇu is incorrect. Its extensive tīrtha list and use of *Jaiva* for Jupiter, absent in Yajñavalkya, establish the extant Viṣṇudharmasūtra as later than both Manusmṛti and Yajñavalkyasmṛti. The older kernel likely dates 300-100 B.C., with additions after the 3rd and before the 7th century A.D., some aligning with Nārada.

Regarding its reception and cultural context, Viśvarūpa references Viṣṇu for saṃnyāsins, though not explicitly quoting its sūtras. While Mītākṣarā rarely quotes it, Aparārka and Smṛticandrikā cite it profusely, suggesting later verse additions. It mentions the seven days of the week, recommends sati, refers to pustakas, and details omens, including the sight of yellow-robed Buddhist ascetics. It prohibits speech with Mlecchas and travel to Mleccha countries, while dilating on Vāsudeva worship. The tradition also acknowledges Bṛhad Viṣṇu, Vṛddha Viṣṇu, and Laghu Viṣṇu, with commentaries by Nandapaṇḍita and likely Bhāruci, attesting to its continued scholarly engagement.
