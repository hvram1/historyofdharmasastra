The complete Smṛti of Bṛhaspati on vyavahāra has not yet been discovered. Bṛhaspati closely follows Manu, often pointedly referring to Manu's text to explain and define its laconic terms. This Smṛtikāra treats of nine ordeals and was the first to clearly distinguish between civil and criminal justice, providing elaborate rules of procedure. There is a close agreement between Nārada and Bṛhaspati on many points. While Bṛhaspati generally agrees closely with Manu, it differs on matters such as the partibility of clothes and the maximum interest rates on corn, fruit, wood, and beasts of burden.

Regarding the age of Bṛhaspati, it is posited to be later than both Manu and Yājñavalkya. The text's use of the words nānaka and dīnāra suggests it is probably of the same age as Nārada. Kātyāyana, whom Viśvarūpa, around 825 A.D., regarded as an ancient sage, frequently refers to the views of Bṛhaspati. Therefore, Dr. Jolly's assignment of Bṛhaspati to the 6th or 7th century is deemed incorrect; Bṛhaspati flourished between 200-400 A.D. The home of Bṛhaspati, however, cannot be determined.

Verses attributed to Bṛhaspati on ācāra, śrāddha, āśauca, and saṁskāra are preserved in works such as the Mitākṣarā and Smṛticandrikā. References are also found to a Vṛddha Bṛhaspati and a Jyotir Bṛhaspati. Professor K. V. Rangaswami Aiyangar undertook the laborious performance of reconstructing Bṛhaspati's smṛti, which was subsequently published in the Gaekwad's Oriental Series, arranged in seven parts. While a significant achievement, some verses and prose passages still remain unnoticed, a point underscored by Professor Renou's criticism against Professor Aiyangar's reconstruction.
