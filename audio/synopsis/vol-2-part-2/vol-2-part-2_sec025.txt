Dāna, or gifts, represents a special and highly extolled feature of the householder's stage, with its significance lauded even in the Ṛgveda. However, certain early works censured the gift of horses, and land gifts were not favoured in very early times. Distinct from rituals such as yāga and homa, the essence of dāna is often encapsulated within the concept of iṣṭāpūrta. All individuals, including women and śūdras, were considered capable of making gifts, though precise criteria existed for persons fit and unfit to be donees. Texts meticulously delineate what things could and could not be donated, classifying articles into three categories, and defining dānas as nitya, naimittika, or kāmya. Making gifts in secret was eulogised, while certain gifts were not to be spurned, and others expressly forbidden. Prescribed proper times included eclipses, saṁkrānti, and ayana days, with a general proscription against night-time giving, alongside guidance on proper places and the presiding deities of various articles, all framed within a general procedure for making gifts.

Kings were particularly required to make various kinds of gifts to brāhmaṇas, with their support for brāhmaṇa marriages and settlements being highly eulogised. Gifts of land were considered the most meritorious, and Smṛti rules regarding land-grants were demonstrably followed in epigraphic records. Verses strongly deprecated the resumption of gifts made by earlier kings, and prior gifts to temples and brāhmaṇas were specifically excepted in grants of villages. Royal grants frequently remitted taxes and detailed the eight bhogas in relation to the land, sparking discussion on whether the king was the sole owner of all lands in the kingdom. Purāṇas extensively describe mahādānas, detailing sixteen such major gifts, including the elaborate procedure of Tulā-puruṣa and others. The gift of cows was highly extolled, alongside ten kinds of dhenu gifts, such as those of ghee and jaggery, and ten parvata or meru dānas, comprising heaps of corn, salt, and sesame.

Beyond these, acts like establishing a pavilion for distributing water, gifting books, making gifts for propitiating planets, and founding hospitals were also forms of dāna. The Dharmaśāstra also addresses the ethical and legal aspects, outlining expiations for accepting gifts which should not have been received, criteria for when a gift becomes irrevocable, and enumerating various kinds of invalid gifts. It is notable that modern courts have often held gifts made merely to dharma as void.
