This chapter offers concise descriptions of various other soma sacrifices, including the Ukthya, Ṣoḍaśin, Atyagniṣṭoma, Atirātra, and Aptoryāma. The Vājapeya, however, stands as an independent sacrifice, profoundly marked by the number seventeen – evident in the seventeen cups of soma and surā for Prajāpati, and in the race involving seventeen chariots and drums. Intended for a brāhmaṇa or kṣatriya desiring super-eminence, its rituals include a chariot race with horses smelling caru, the brāhmā priest chanting the Vājī-sāman, and an udumbara post as the goal. The sacrificer's chariot leads, followed by others, before returning to the sacrificial ground. The pratiprasthātṛ consumes the principal wine cup, while other participants drink the remaining sixteen. A unique rite involves the sacrificer climbing a ladder against the yūpa for a dialogue with his wife. Substantial dakṣiṇās, such as 1700 cows and 17 chariots, are distributed. Post-Vājapeya, a king performs the Rājasūya, and a brāhmaṇa the Bṛhaspatisava. Jaimini's conclusions on Vājapeya are also noted, alongside brief mention of Ekāha sacrifices like Viśvajit, Gosava, and Sarvasvāra, and Ahina sacrifices, exemplified by the Dvādaśāha, noting its variations.

The Rājasūya, a distinct and highly intricate ceremony reserved solely for kṣatriyas, spans over two years, integrating numerous iṣṭis, soma, and animal sacrifices. Its Dīkṣā initiates on the first day of the bright half of Phālguna, culminating in the central Abhiṣecaṇīya, or consecration rite, a year later on the first of Caitra, which follows Ukthya procedure. Key features include the use of seventeen kinds of waters for anointing, recitation of āvid formulae and the Śunaḥśepa story, symbolic acts like Viṣṇukramas and a rigged dice-play, and the elaborate Daśāpeya with its substantial dakṣiṇās. A comprehensive year of post-ceremonial observances concludes this extensive ritual cycle, involving keśavaraṇīya, Vyuṣṭi-dvirātra, Kṣatradhṛti, and Sautrāmaṇī iṣṭi.
