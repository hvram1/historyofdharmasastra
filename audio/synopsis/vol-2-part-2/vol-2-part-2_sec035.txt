The Sautrāmaṇī, identified by Gautama as one of the seven Hāviryajñas, is notably characterized by the offering of *surā*, or wine, a practice now commonly substituted with milk. This four-day ritual, encompassing both Kokilī and Caraka-sautrāmaṇī forms, involved the elaborate preparation of wine, culminating in offerings of milk, wine, and the sacrifice of goats; remnants of the *surā* were not consumed by priests but carefully disposed of. The Aśvamedha, an ancient royal horse-sacrifice referenced even in the *R̥gveda*, was a three-day rite performed by kings. Following a year during which a consecrated horse roamed, it was ceremonially killed after the sacrificer took *dīkṣā*. This complex ceremony featured significant involvement of the queens, unique dialogues, the *brahmodya*, and an *avabhr̥tha* bath believed to cleanse grave sins, despite its rarity even in antiquity.

Sattras are communal sacrifices varying in duration from twelve days to over a year, with the Dvādaśāha serving as their archetype and the Gavām-ayana modelling year-long forms. In a *sattra*, all participants function as both *yajamānas* and priests, though one assumes the role of *gṛhapati*. The Mahāvrata, the penultimate day, is particularly noted for its unique social interactions, including praise and abuse between *brāhmaṇas* and *śūdras*, mock fights, and abusive dialogues involving harlots and *brahmacārins*. Jaimini, among ancient writers, considered thousand-year *sattras* mythical, interpreting *saṃvatsara* in such descriptions to mean 'a day'. The Agnicayana, conversely, stands as the most intricate and recondite of all *śrauta* sacrifices, deeply rooted in cosmological conceptions as elucidated by the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*. It involves the meticulous, multi-layered construction of a fire altar, an *aṅga* of the Somayāga, over a foundation where a living tortoise is enveloped in moss, utilizing specific bricks and numerous offerings throughout its prolonged process.
