Sacrifices are broadly divided into iṣṭi, paśu, and soma, with Agniṣṭoma, often identified with Jyotiṣṭoma, representing a primary five-day soma sacrifice. It commences with inviting priests honoured with Madhuparka, and securing the devayajana from the king. The sacrificer and his wife undergo apsu-dīkṣā, purified with darbhas, and through dīkṣaṇīyā iṣṭi, become dīkṣita, a status that elevates even a kṣatriya to a brāhmaṇa for the ritual. This phase includes specific observances, silence, and the prāyaṇīyā iṣṭi. The ceremonial purchase of Soma follows, with its stalks transported to the prāgvamśa amidst the Subrahmaṇyā litany. After Ātithyeṣṭi and the Tanūnaptrs covenant, significant rites like Pravargya, believed to endow a new body, and Upasad are performed, while the Mahāvedi, uttaravedi, nābhi, sadas, and dhiṣṇyas are meticulously prepared.

The final day, known as 'sutyā', begins with the Prātaranuvāka prayer, the critical extraction of Soma, and the Mahābhiṣava or principal pressing. Soma is offered from various cups to deities, accompanied by the chanting of the Bahispavamāna laud and other stotras and śastras across morning, mid-day, and evening pressings. Animal and cake offerings are made, culminating in the distribution of dakṣiṇā. The elaborate ritual concludes with the Avabhṛtha, the final purifying bath, where vessels are immersed, and the Avabhṛtha sāman is chanted. The yajamāna and his wife complete the ceremonies with concluding iṣṭis such as the Udayanīyā and Anubandhyā. Theories regarding the soma plant's identity, its lunar connection, and the use of substitutes like 'rānśera' in the Deccan, underscore its profound significance.
