Samnyāsa, the ascetic order, involves giving up worldly ties, begging, and contemplation on the Absolute, a practice rooted in the earliest Upaniṣads, with the Jābālopaniṣad prescribing its rules. Dharmasūtras and smṛtis detail its salient features, identifying Tridaṇḍī and Ekadaṇḍī ascetics, and four kinds: Kuṭīcaka, Bahūdaka, Haṁsa, and Paramahaṁsa. Ancient texts, however, refute the popular notion that a Paramahaṁsa is beyond all rules. Other categories include Vidvat-samnyāsa, Vividiṣā-samnyāsa, Turīyātīta, and Avadhūta. While primarily for brāhmaṇas, śūdras were generally prohibited by smṛtis; women, in rare cases, adopted this life.

The chapter examines the ten Advaita samnyāsin orders following Śamkarācārya, detailing disputes over matha properties, ecclesiastical jurisdiction, and successor appointments. It notes the medieval deviation where some samnyāsins abandoned ahiṁsā. A samnyāsin is legally severed from family, losing property rights, though certain Gosāvis, by custom, maintained wives and concubines. The elaborate Samnyāsa procedure, detailed in sūtras and Dharmasindhu, involves specific śrāddhas, sāvitrīpraveśa, virājahoma, a declaration of renunciation and ahiṁsā, and relinquishing śikhā and yajñopavīta. A guru teaches pañcīkaraṇa and mahāvākyas, bestows a new name, yogapaṭṭa, and initiates paryaṅka-śauca. Samnyāsa in extremis and related controversies are also discussed, observing no impurity for relatives on an ascetic's death.

Modern matha heads claim jurisdiction over caste matters, excommunication, and expiations, a role historically fulfilled by pariṣads, whose advice kings, such as Shivaji, sought through his Panditrao. The chapter concludes by noting that while asceticism shares many features with other religions, the perception of a universally high regard for ascetics in India is a partial truth.
