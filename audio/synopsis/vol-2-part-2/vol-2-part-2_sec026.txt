Chapter XXVI delves into Pratiṣṭhā and Utsarga, encompassing the founding of temples and the dedication of public utilities such as wells and tanks. It notes that women and śūdras could contribute to pūrta-dharma, unlike iṣṭa or Vedic sacrifices, with charitable works for public benefit eventually being considered more meritorious than sacrificial rites. The text traces the evolution of procedures for dedicating tanks and wells, from earlier sūtra prescriptions to those gradually superseded by purāṇas. It clarifies the specific meanings of dāna, pratiṣṭhā, and utsarga, and highlights the high cultural value placed on trees in ancient India, which were believed to save a man from hell just as a son did, thereby justifying tree worship.

The chapter proceeds to detail the consecration of images in temples, whether public or private. It outlines the procedure according to the Matsya-purāṇa, noting the later incorporation of details from Tantra works, including the three kinds of Nyāsas: mātṛkānyāsa, tattvanyāsa, and mantra-nyāsa. The consecration of a Viṣṇu image, as per the Vaikhānasa Smārtasūtra, is also discussed. The practice of attaching dancing girls to temples is identified as comparatively ancient, and the circumstances necessitating punaḥ-pratiṣṭhā, or re-consecration, are explained. Jīrṇoddhāra, the repairing or reconstructing of dilapidated temples, along with its time and procedure, is also covered.

The discourse extends to the founding of maṭhas—monasteries or colleges for teachers and pupils—distinguishing them from temples. It acknowledges the maṭhas established by Śamkarācārya and explores their general origin, including the devolution of their property, and the appointment and powers of their heads. Historically, rulers and courts exercised control over the administration of temple and maṭha properties, a legacy that finds continuity in modern legislation dealing with religious and charitable endowments. The concept of Yogakṣema being impartible is mentioned, alongside the founder's control over dedicated public works, and the powers of a shebait to remove or establish an idol.
