A profound understanding of Vedic sacrifices is fundamental for properly comprehending Vedic Literature and appreciating its widespread influence on the varṇas. While their precise chronology remains uncertain, a considerable body of works, both ancient and modern, exists on Vedic sacrifices. Jaimini's insights, in particular, are crucial for the interpretation of Vedic texts pertaining to these rituals. Evidence suggests the cult of yajña was prevalent as early as the Indo-Iranian period, with literary and epigraphic records confirming the continued performance of Vedic sacrifices by kings even after the advent of Buddha. Furthermore, historical grants made by monarchs attest to their patronage, enabling brāhmaṇas to perform essential rites such as agnihotra. Even the Ṛgveda contains numerous references to sacrificial matters, indicating their ancient roots.

These elaborate rituals were governed by general rules applicable across all sūtras. The performance involved specific mantras of four kinds: ṛk, yajus, sāman, and nigada, alongside the use of distinct ladles and various other sacrificial utensils. Central to the performance were the several fires, established through the five bhūsaṁskāras and the rite of agnyādheya. This initiation involved carefully choosing the devayajana, or place of worship, and following a precise procedure for agnyādheya, sometimes followed by punarādheya. Among the daily rites, agnihotra, performed both morning and evening, held particular significance, with specific rules detailing its observance even when the householder traveled away from home, whether alone or accompanied by his wife.
