The Mitākṣarā holds a unique position, esteemed as an authority of paramount importance, particularly by the British Indian courts. Known by several names, this monumental work quotes a host of smṛti writers and six predecessors, including authors of commentaries and digests. Noticeable features of the Mitākṣarā include its measured approach, being chary of quoting from Purāṇas, alongside its practice of mentioning the views of many Smṛti writers with the prefixes ‘bṛhat’ or ‘vṛddha’. Examples such as Bṛhan-Manu, Vṛddha-Manu, Bṛhad-Vasiṣṭha, and Vṛddha-Viṣṇu appear frequently, especially when discussing topics like āśauca and prāyaścitta.

Turning to the author, Vijñāneshvara's personal history reveals him as a profound student of pūrvamīmāṁsā, with his date generally placed between 1100 and 1120. It is crucial to note that Dr. Derrett's facile assumption about Vijñāneshvara being a judge, based on the Mitākṣarā's commentary on Yājñavalkya II. 4, is not acceptable. Similarly, criticism is directed towards Professor R. Aiyangar for his hasty and biased remark suggesting that the Mitākṣarā refers to Lakṣmīdhara's Kalpataru in its concluding verses.

Among the numerous commentators of the Mitākṣarā, three have achieved particular fame, and the text is also known for its peculiar doctrines. Vijñāneshvara also appears to have authored the Āśaucardasaka, a work which subsequently saw several commentaries by scholars such as Harihara, Raghunātha, and Bhaṭṭoji. However, it is established that Vijñāneshvara was not the author of the Trimśat-śloki. Significantly, Nārāyaṇa, a pupil of Vijñāneshvara, is credited with writing the Vyavahāra-śiromaṇi.
